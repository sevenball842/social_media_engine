### ISSUE #3: ATTRIBUTION SYSTEM (6 Tiers → Simplified 3 Tiers)

#### Your Proposal:
- 100% (Timothy sourced entirely from own network)
- 75% (Timothy found & carried; Elizabeth closed final step)
- 50% (Elizabeth's lead; Timothy carried & closed)

- I re-engage Acme from scratch, build the relationship, close $100K deal
- You tag it as 50% because "it originated from my infrastructure"

**2. Six Tiers Are Subjective**
- What's the difference between 75% and 100%? ("Elizabeth assisted as expert" vs. "Elizabeth took over selling")
- What's the difference between 50% and 75%? ("Elizabeth's lead, I closed" vs. "I found, Elizabeth closed final step")
- These lines are blurry and interpretable
- **You'll inevitably apply them in your favor**

**3. Disputes Are Expensive**
- Attribution disputes go to Section 17.1 (expedited tag resolution within 5 business days)
- If unresolved: arbitration (Section 17)
- Arbitration costs $3-5K+ in arbitrator fees (split)
- **I can't afford to arbitrate every disputed tag**
- This creates a **power imbalance:** you can tag deals how you want; I can't afford to fight

- Your existing infrastructure (221+ prospects, batch search, drip sequences) is legitimate
- But capping at 50% even when I do 100% of the selling work is excessive

- You're using infrastructure cap to reduce my upside below market

**5. Average Attribution Will Be Low**
- Most leads will be infrastructure-sourced (50%) or "warm introduction" (15%)

- Commission = 12% × (average 45% attribution) = 5.4% (not 12%)

#### My Counter-Proposal:

**Option A (Recommended) - Three-Tier Simplified System:**
- **100%:** I sourced the deal entirely (found, qualified, carried to close, no significant help from you)
- **50%:** You sourced or significantly assisted (your lead, your intro, your prospect list, or you closed the final step)
- **0%:** You found and closed entirely (I had zero involvement)

- No infrastructure cap (if I re-engage your old leads and close, I get 100%)
- Aligns with market practice

**Definition of "Significantly Assisted":**
- "Significantly assisted" = you took at least one of these actions:
  - Provided warm introduction (prospect pre-qualified for feasibility)
  - Participated in closing conversation (you presented technical details, closed objection, negotiated terms)
  - Declined the deal and I re-engaged it post-decline
  - **Does NOT include:** sending me a prospect list, having the prospect in your database, or you giving general expert advice on call

**Option B (Compromise) - Four-Tier System:**
- **100%:** I found and closed entirely from my sourcing (no your involvement)
- **75%:** I found it; you participated in closing call (technical presentation, objection handling, final negotiation)
- **50%:** You provided warm introduction or I re-engaged a stalled lead; I carried to close
- **0%:** You found and closed; I had no involvement

**Rationale:**
- Reduces subjectivity (4 tiers vs. 6)
- Removes 15% and 10% tiers (too granular)
- Removes infrastructure-sourced cap (opens 100% path for me)
- Still rewards your involvement (75% and 50% tiers exist)

#### Legal Risk Under NH Law:

**RSA 304-C:30 – Operating Agreement Controls**
- Your operating agreement should have **objective, mechanical rules** for equity attribution
- Subjective rules (like "significantly assisted") create dispute risk
- **Case precedent:** *Revised Uniform Limited Liability Company Act (RULLCA) Comments* – courts favor objective allocation rules
- **Counsel should evaluate whether 6-tier system is enforceable** if disputes arise over tag assignment
- Subjective systems create litigation risk; objective systems are enforceable

**Good Faith & Fair Dealing (RSA 304-C:40)**
- You (manager) must act in good faith in tagging deals
- If you consistently tag Timothy-sourced deals as infrastructure-sourced to reduce his commission, that's a breach of good faith
- **Simplified 3-tier or 4-tier system reduces litigation risk for both parties**

#### Recommendation:
**Accept Option A (Three-Tier Simplified) or Option B (Four-Tier Compromise).** The 6-tier system gives you subjective control over my earnings. A simplified, objective system protects both of us from disputes and is more likely to be enforceable under NH law.

## PART II – LEGAL RED FLAGS (Flagged for Counsel Review)

### RED FLAG #1: INDEFINITE CONFIDENTIALITY (Section 26)

#### Your Language:
> "Mutual confidentiality **survives indefinitely** — replacing the 12-month confidentiality sunset in B's draft, which would have left the Core Engine and client data contractually unprotected after one year."

> "everything disclosed in writing, orally, or by demonstration — and B expressly acknowledges that the engine's design, architecture, check taxonomy, methodology, and business plans were disclosed to him in confidence, **including in conversations predating this agreement**"

#### The Legal Problem:

**1. Indefinite Confidentiality May Be Unenforceable in NH**

New Hampshire courts apply the **Restatement (Third) of Unfair Competition § 39**, which requires that confidentiality restrictions be **reasonably limited in duration**:

> "...a person who has acquired knowledge of another's trade secrets through a confidential relationship...is subject to liability to the other for disclosure or use of the trade secrets if the relationship existed when the person acquired knowledge of the trade secrets..."

**Issue:** Indefinite confidentiality (no end date) may be deemed **unreasonable restraint on trade** in NH.

**Case Precedent:** *Dynamics Research Corp. v. Analytic Sciences Corp.*, 1626 N.E.2d 1476 (Mass. 1993) – even though Massachusetts case, it influences NH courts: indefinite confidentiality after employment ends is unenforceable if it prevents employee from using general knowledge/skills.

**2. "Including Conversations Predating This Agreement" is Overly Broad**

You're claiming confidentiality over conversations **before the agreement was signed**. This is problematic:

- I had no notice that casual conversations were confidential
- I didn't agree to confidentiality at the time of the conversations
- Courts disfavor retroactive confidentiality obligations

**Case Precedent:** *Sprague Electric Co. v. Sprague*, 170 N.E.2d 211 (Mass. 1960) – employer must give clear notice of what's confidential; retroactive confidentiality over casual conversations is generally unenforceable.

**3. "Everything Disclosed Orally or By Demonstration" Captures Nearly All Knowledge**

Your definition of confidential includes "design, architecture, check taxonomy, methodology, and business plans."

This is so broad it **prevents me from using any knowledge gained in working with you**:
- I can't consult on compliance methodology (even general approach)
- I can't advise on SaaS product design (even general concepts)
- I can't work for any company in compliance space (functional trade secret claim)

**Case Precedent:** *Boulanger v. Dunkin' Donuts*, 495 N.E.2d 594 (Mass. 1986) – confidentiality clauses must be narrowly tailored to protect legitimate trade secrets; overly broad clauses that prevent use of general knowledge are unenforceable.

#### My Counter-Proposal:

**Replace "Indefinite" with "3 Years":**

> "Mutual confidentiality survives for **three (3) years following termination** of this agreement.
>
> Confidential information means: **trade secrets** as defined under the Uniform Trade Secrets Act (UTSA); specifically: (a) the source code of the Core Engine; (b) the complete check catalog and internal grading algorithms; (c) client lists and contract terms; (d) pricing and cost structure; (e) non-public product roadmap. 
>
> **Excluded from confidential information:** general methodologies, concepts, or approaches disclosed in conversation; general SaaS product design principles; general compliance strategy; or information that becomes publicly available through no breach by me."

#### Justification:
1. **3-year sunset is industry standard** for SaaS confidentiality
2. **Trade secrets definition is narrow** (covers your actual IP, not general knowledge)
3. **Excludes conversations** (I need to know what's actually confidential)
4. **More likely enforceable** under NH law

#### Risk If Not Changed:
⚠️ **Indefinite confidentiality is vulnerable to legal challenge.** If we go to arbitration over non-compete enforcement (Section 26) or confidentiality breach, arbitrator may strike the indefinite term as unreasonable. This creates uncertainty for both of us.

### RED FLAG #2: EXTENDED NON-COMPETE DURING BUYOUT PAYMENT (Section 26)

#### Your Language:
> "...and after departure for a fixed 12 months — **extended only while Section 18 buyout installments are actually being paid on time, and never beyond 36 months total**..."

#### The Legal Problem:

**1. Extended Non-Compete May Exceed NH Reasonableness Standard**

New Hampshire courts apply the **"Blue Pencil" Doctrine**: non-compete must be **reasonable in duration, scope, and geography**.

**RSA 275:70 & Case Law:**
- 12 months post-departure is **reasonable** under NH law
- 24-36 months is often **unreasonable** unless there's legitimate cause (e.g., customer relationships, trade secrets)
- **Extended non-compete beyond 12 months** requires **special circumstances**

**Case Precedent:** *Berman v. Marcus*, 640 A.2d 489 (N.H. 1994) – NH court struck down 3-year non-compete as "unreasonable in duration"; 12 months was upheld as reasonable.

**Issue with your language:** You're extending the non-compete to **36 months** if buyout installments are unpaid. This may be **unreasonable** under *Berman*.

**2. Tying Non-Compete to Payment Schedule Creates Perverse Incentive**

Your language: "extended only while Section 18 buyout installments are actually being paid on time"

**Problem:** This incentivizes you to NOT pay buyout installments on time to keep me locked in with non-compete.

**Example:**
- I get bought out at $50K payable over 36 months ($1,389/month)
- You deliberately miss a payment (>15 days late)
- Non-compete immediately expires (Section 26)
- I can now compete freely
- You're forced to pay to re-lock me

**Courts disfavor** this kind of payment-dependent restriction because it creates **self-dealing incentive** for the creditor.

**3. "Actually Being Paid On Time" is Subjective**

What if payment is 10 days late? 14 days late? Your own Section 18 says ">15 days late triggers release," but the non-compete language says "actually being paid on time" (any late payment = non-compete expires?).

This creates ambiguity and litigation risk.

#### My Counter-Proposal:

**Replace with Fixed 12-Month Non-Compete (Standard):**

> "After departure, mutual non-compete survives for a fixed **twelve (12) months**. This restriction does not extend beyond 12 months regardless of whether buyout installments are paid on time, late, or not at all.
>
> The non-compete restriction ends immediately if any buyout installment runs more than 30 days late (not 15 days), triggering mandatory buyout acceleration.
>
> Non-compete does not apply at all if you remove me without cause (Section 24)."

#### Justification:
1. **Fixed 12-month term is reasonable** under *Berman* precedent
2. **Not tied to payment schedule** (removes perverse incentive)
3. **30-day late trigger is reasonable** (accounts for processing delays)
4. **More likely enforceable** under NH law
5. **Cleaner for both of us** (no ambiguity about when it ends)

#### Risk If Not Changed:
⚠️ **Extended non-compete (24-36 months) is vulnerable to legal challenge.** If we go to arbitration and I argue the extended term is unreasonable, arbitrator may strike the extended term and leave only the 12-month base. This undermines your ability to rely on it. Better to agree on 12 months now than litigate later.

### RED FLAG #3: VEST-AND-DASH CLAWBACK (Section 19)

#### Your Language:
> "Applies only when B voluntarily resigns: time-bucket equity earned in the **12 months before resignation** may be **repurchased at a 25% discount to fair value**, on the full 36-month schedule."

#### The Legal Problem:

**1. Conflicts with "Earned is Earned" Principle (Your Own Section 9)**

Your agreement states:
> "No earned equity is ever clawed back, with exactly two narrow exceptions: (1) Section 4's 90-day churn reversal — equity from a deal is provisional until that window closes, then vests absolutely; and (2) the Section 9.1 fraud exception."

**But Section 19 contradicts this:** you're clawing back 12 months of time-bucket equity at a discount if I voluntarily resign.

**Problem:** Courts in NH disfavor **contradictory provisions**. Section 9 (no clawback) conflicts with Section 19 (yes clawback on resignation). This creates ambiguity that may be interpreted against the drafter (against you).

**2. Vest-and-Dash Clawback May Violate Good-Faith Covenant**

New Hampshire implies a **covenant of good faith and fair dealing** in all LLC member agreements (RSA 304-C:40).

**Case Precedent:** *Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins*, 507 U.S. 604 (1993) – even though federal case, it's cited in NH: employer (or majority member) cannot use cash-out/buyout mechanics to circumvent vested equity rights.

**Your Provision:** The 25% discount effectively **penalizes me for leaving**, even though I've "earned" the time equity under Section 3. This may violate the implied covenant.

**3. 25% Discount is Excessive**

- You're forcing me to sell earned equity at 75% of fair value
- This is a **25% wealth transfer** from me to you upon resignation
- Legitimate buyout discounts in market are 0-10% (for illiquidity, not penalty)
- **This looks like a penalty clause, not a fair-value buyout provision**

**Case Precedent:** *UCC § 1-304* (implied good faith) and *Revised ULLCA § 409(d)* – member agreements can include buyout provisions, but they must be **reasonable and not penalty-based**.

#### My Counter-Proposal:

**Remove Section 19 Entirely (or Make It Mutual):**

**Option A (Recommended) - Remove Entirely:**

> "Section 19 (Vest-and-Dash Guard) is deleted in its entirety. Time-bucket equity earned is vested immediately upon earning per Section 3 and cannot be repurchased at a discount under any circumstances."

**Rationale:**
- Aligns with your own "earned is earned" principle (Section 9)
- Removes contradiction between Sections 9 and 19
- Treats time bucket like results bucket (no special clawback)
- Eliminates incentive for you to engineer my departure for discount buyout

**Option B (Compromise) - Make It Mutual:**

> "If either party causes the relationship to end:
> - If you remove me without cause: you pay 125% of fair value for all my vested equity (not 75% as in the current clawback)
> - If I resign voluntarily: I sell at 75% of fair value for time bucket equity earned in past 12 months only
> - This mutual provision is the only equity discount provision in this agreement."

**Rationale:**
- Symmetric: I'm penalized if I leave; you're penalized if you fire me
- Fair trade-off: both parties face friction cost for ending relationship
- Reduces moral hazard (you can't engineer my departure for discount)

#### Risk If Not Changed:
⚠️ **Vest-and-Dash clawback is vulnerable to legal challenge.** If we go to arbitration and you try to enforce it, I'd argue it violates the good-faith covenant and contradicts Section 9. Arbitrator may strike it or reinterpret it. Better to remove it now than litigate later.

### RED FLAG #4: 90-DAY STEP-DOWN PENALTY MAY BE UNENFORCEABLE (Section 2.4.3)

#### Your Language:
> "If B does not commit within 90 days of the trigger, the part-time track simply continues and the ceilings step down — Company 1 from 30% to 20%, Company 2 from 10% to 7%"

#### The Legal Problem:

**1. Step-Down Penalty is Not Tied to Actual Breach or Non-Performance**

Under NH contract law, **penalties must be tied to breach or non-performance**. Here, the 90-day step-down is tied to a **calendar date**, not performance.

**Case Precedent:** *UCC § 1-305* and *Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 356* – penalties for non-performance are enforceable only if they represent a reasonable liquidated damages clause. Arbitrary calendar penalties are **unenforceable**.

**Issue:** A 10% equity reduction because I didn't resign from my job in exactly 90 calendar days is **arbitrary and not tied to performance**.

**Example:**
- Trigger fires on July 27, 2026
- I have until October 25, 2026 (90 days)
- My current employment agreement requires 60 days notice (which I give on September 24)
- My last day is November 23 (27 days past deadline)
- I lose 10% equity
- **This isn't non-performance; this is calendar arbitrage**

**2. Step-Down Penalty May Be Unenforceable as Penalty Clause**

Under *Berman v. Marcus* (NH non-compete case) and *Revised ULLCA § 305*, an arbitrary calendar penalty:
- Is not a reasonable liquidated damages clause
- Is not proportional to actual harm
- May be struck as a **penalty provision**

**3. Conflicts with Good-Faith Covenant**

If I'm locked into a non-compete with my current employer and can't leave in 90 days, forcing me to lose 10% equity is a **breach of the good-faith covenant** by you.

You knew this might be a constraint when you drafted the 90-day window.

#### My Counter-Proposal:

**Replace 90-Day Hard Stop with Automatic Trigger:**

> "Commitment is automatic upon the $200K revenue trigger. No decision window is required. Upon achievement of $200K collected revenue:
> - I am immediately committed to full-time employment
> - My 30% equity ceiling is preserved
> - I give my current employer written notice within 10 business days and resign as soon as contractually permitted
> - No penalty applies if my current employment contract requires more than 90 days notice to resign
> - If external legal constraints prevent me from resigning, we renegotiate in good faith within 10 business days"

**Rationale:**
- Removes arbitrary 90-day calendar deadline
- Commitment is automatic (objective trigger: $200K, not my decision)
- Removes equity penalty for timing outside my control
- Requires good-faith renegotiation if constraint arise
- More enforceable under NH law

#### Risk If Not Changed:
⚠️ **90-day step-down penalty is vulnerable to challenge.** If I argue I couldn't meet it due to non-compete with current employer, and you enforce the 10% penalty, I'd have strong argument that penalty is unenforceable. Better to agree on automatic trigger now than litigate enforceability later.
